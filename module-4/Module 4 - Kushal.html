📡 The Air Around You Is Not Empty

You're sitting somewhere right now. Maybe a coffee shop. Maybe your bedroom. Maybe a university library. Close your eyes and picture the air around you. It looks empty. It is not.

Right now, invisible radio waves are passing through your body carrying fragments of other people's conversations, passwords, emails, and login sessions. Your neighbour's router is broadcasting its network name — its SSID — on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz simultaneously, advertising its existence to every device within 100 metres. Including yours. Including an attacker's.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the password protecting your home Wi-Fi is probably in a file that anyone can download. A 10 GB wordlist called "rockyou.txt" contains 14,344,391 real passwords leaked from actual data breaches. If your Wi-Fi password is a word, a name, a date, or anything a human would naturally choose — it's almost certainly in that file. A laptop running aircrack-ng will crack it in under 60 seconds.

And that's just the beginning. WEP — the original Wi-Fi encryption standard — can be cracked in under 60 seconds with freely available tools. WPA2 (what most of your devices use today) has a critical vulnerability in its handshake that lets an attacker capture the authentication exchange without ever knowing the password first. WPA3 is the fix — but most routers still run WPA2. This module is the story of how we got here, and how attackers exploit every step of the journey.

01 / 04 The Arms Race

WEP → WPA2 → WPA3:
A History of Breaking Things

Wi-Fi security is a story of cryptographers building locks and other cryptographers picking them. Each generation of wireless security protocol was considered unbreakable when it launched. Each one eventually fell. Understanding how each one broke tells you exactly what the next one had to fix.

1997
WEP COMPLETELY BROKEN

Wired Equivalent Privacy — the original Wi-Fi encryption standard, designed to provide the same privacy as a wired connection. It used the RC4 stream cipher with a 40-bit key. Sounds reasonable for 1997.

The catastrophic flaw: WEP reused its Initialisation Vectors (IVs) — short 24-bit random values that are supposed to make each encrypted packet unique. With only 16 million possible IVs and a busy network generating thousands of packets per second, IVs repeat within minutes. An attacker captures enough packets with repeated IVs, runs statistical analysis, and recovers the encryption key entirely — regardless of its length.

CRACK TIME: Under 60 seconds with airodump-ng + aircrack-ng on any modern laptop
TOOL: aircrack-ng, airreplay-ng — freely available, runs on a Raspberry Pi
2003
WPA / TKIP DEPRECATED / WEAK

Wi-Fi Protected Access with TKIP (Temporal Key Integrity Protocol) was an emergency patch for WEP — designed to run on existing hardware while fixing the IV reuse problem. TKIP generates a new key for every packet using a mixing function.

It fixed WEP's immediate flaw but introduced new ones. The Michael MIC algorithm used for integrity checking was vulnerable to forgery attacks. TKIP was deprecated in 2012 and is now considered broken. Still, it was never as catastrophically weak as WEP.

VULNERABILITY: MIC forgery, TKIP packet injection — requires captured traffic, less trivial than WEP
STATUS: Deprecated — devices advertising TKIP-only should be avoided entirely
2004
WPA2 / CCMP / AES STRONG — BUT HANDSHAKE IS CAPTURABLE

WPA2 replaced TKIP with CCMP (Counter Mode with CBC-MAC Protocol) built on AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) — the same cipher used to protect classified government data. The encryption itself has never been broken.

The vulnerability isn't in the encryption — it's in the 4-way handshake that establishes the connection. This handshake can be captured by anyone in monitor mode within radio range. Once captured, it can be subjected to offline dictionary attacks. If the password is in a wordlist, it cracks. The encryption is perfect. The password is the problem.

VULNERABILITY: KRACK attack (2017) — Key Reinstallation Attack on the 4-way handshake
OFFLINE CRACK: Captured handshake + dictionary/brute force — no interaction with AP needed
2018
WPA3 / SAE CURRENT BEST PRACTICE

WPA3 replaces the vulnerable 4-way handshake with SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals), also known as the Dragonfly handshake. SAE provides forward secrecy — even if an attacker captures the handshake AND later learns the password, they cannot decrypt previously captured traffic.

WPA3 also eliminates offline dictionary attacks entirely: the authentication is interactive, requiring the attacker to communicate with the AP in real time for each guess — making large-scale cracking computationally infeasible. The major limitation is adoption: most deployed routers still run WPA2.

FORWARD SECRECY: Yes — session keys cannot be derived from captured handshakes
OFFLINE CRACK: Eliminated — authentication requires live interaction with the AP

The WPA2 4-Way Handshake —
Why It Can Be Captured

WPA2's encryption is unbreakable. But to establish an encrypted connection, the client and access point must agree on a shared session key — without transmitting it directly. They do this through the 4-way handshake, a cryptographic exchange that proves both sides know the password without ever sending it. The problem: the entire exchange happens over the air, in plaintext-ish frames, and any device in monitor mode can capture it.

💡 The Deauthentication Trick

The attacker doesn't need to wait for a client to naturally connect. They can send a deauthentication frame (a management frame that Wi-Fi has no way to authenticate) to force any connected client to disconnect. The client immediately tries to reconnect — generating a fresh handshake that the attacker captures. The entire process takes under 10 seconds.

02 / 04 Identity Theft — For Routers

Evil Twin & Rogue APs —
The Network That Isn't Yours

Your phone and laptop are trusting. When they scan for Wi-Fi networks, they look for a network name (SSID) they recognise. If they see "CoffeeShop_WiFi" and they've connected to it before, many devices will connect automatically — without any further verification. They don't check the router's identity. They don't verify a certificate. They just connect.

An Evil Twin attack exploits this trust completely. An attacker creates a fake access point with the exact same SSID as a legitimate network. They typically broadcast at higher power to appear as the stronger signal. Victim devices — especially those configured to auto-connect — connect to the attacker's network instead of the real one. From this position, the attacker can intercept all traffic, serve a captive portal to steal credentials, or perform SSL stripping (Module 2).

A Rogue Access Point is the insider threat version: an unauthorised AP plugged into a corporate network, bypassing all perimeter security. Any employee who connects to it hands the attacker a foothold inside the internal network, completely bypassing the firewall.

Evil Twin AP

The Impersonator

A fake access point broadcasting the same SSID as a legitimate network to intercept connections

Tools like hostapd-wpe, airbase-ng, or Bettercap can spin up a fake AP in seconds on any laptop with a compatible Wi-Fi adapter. Combined with a DHCP server and a captive portal page, the attacker can present a convincing Wi-Fi login page that harvests credentials the moment the victim connects. The victim sees a familiar network name. They never suspect.

Deauthentication Attack

The Forceful Reconnect

Sending spoofed 802.11 management frames to disconnect clients from their legitimate AP

802.11 management frames — including deauthentication frames — are unauthenticated by design in WPA2. Any device can send a deauth frame pretending to be the AP, and the client will disconnect. This is used in two ways: to force a WPA2 handshake capture, or to make a client disconnect from the real network and connect to the Evil Twin instead. WPA3 with Management Frame Protection (MFP) finally addresses this.

Rogue AP

The Inside Job

An unauthorised access point connected to a corporate network, bypassing perimeter defences

A disgruntled employee or a compromised hardware supply chain can introduce a rogue AP — a small device plugged into a network port — that broadcasts a Wi-Fi network accessible from outside the building. Attackers can sit in the car park and gain direct access to the internal corporate network, completely bypassing the perimeter firewall. Detection requires wireless intrusion detection systems (WIDS) that scan for unauthorised SSIDs and BSSIDs.

Defence: WIDS + 802.11w

The Shield

Wireless Intrusion Detection System · IEEE 802.11w Management Frame Protection

WIDS continuously scans the RF environment for unknown SSIDs, duplicate BSSIDs, deauthentication floods, and other attack signatures. Enterprise solutions (Cisco, Aruba, Meraki) alert security teams within seconds of a rogue AP appearing. 802.11w (Management Frame Protection, enabled by default in WPA3) cryptographically signs management frames — making deauth spoofing impossible. Always enable it on WPA2 networks if the AP supports it.

03 / 04 Beyond Wi-Fi

Bluetooth & IoT —
The Forgotten Attack Surface

Wi-Fi is only one of the wireless protocols filling the air around you. Bluetooth connects your headphones, keyboard, smartwatch, and phone. Zigbee connects your smart lights and thermostats. Z-Wave connects your smart locks. Every one of these protocols has had serious vulnerabilities — and most IoT devices never receive security updates.

The IoT (Internet of Things) attack surface is massive and largely undefended. A 2020 study found that 57% of IoT devices are vulnerable to medium- or high-severity attacks, and most run embedded Linux with default credentials that are publicly listed in manufacturer documentation — and never changed by users.

🦷

BlueSnarfing

Bluetooth Classic — OBEX Protocol

An attacker connects to a Bluetooth device without authorisation and extracts its contacts, messages, calendar entries, and files via the OBEX (Object Exchange) protocol. Affects devices with older Bluetooth implementations (pre-2.1). Requires the victim device to be discoverable — most modern devices default to non-discoverable when not actively pairing, but many users never check.

🔑

KRACK Attack

WPA2 — Key Reinstallation

Discovered in 2017, KRACK (Key Reinstallation Attack) exploits the WPA2 handshake's retransmission mechanism. By replaying handshake messages, an attacker forces the client to reinstall an already-used key, resetting nonces and replay counters. This can allow decryption and replay of traffic. Primarily affects clients, not APs. Patched in all major OS updates — but unpatched IoT devices remain vulnerable forever.

🏠

Default Credentials

IoT — Authentication Failure

The most mundane and most exploited vulnerability: IoT devices ship with default usernames and passwords (admin/admin, root/root, admin/1234). Mirai — the botnet that generated the 1.2 Tbps DDoS in 2016 — infected 600,000 devices using exactly 62 default credential combinations. No exploit needed. Just a list and patience.

📡

BlueJacking / Bluebugging

Bluetooth — Unsolicited Access

Bluejacking sends unsolicited messages to nearby discoverable Bluetooth devices — mostly annoying. Bluebugging is more serious: it establishes a hidden connection to the target device and uses AT commands to make calls, send messages, and access the phone book — essentially full remote control of the phone. Affected Nokia, Sony Ericsson, and early smartphones in 2004-2006.

⚡

Zigbee / Z-Wave Attacks

Smart Home Protocols

Zigbee and Z-Wave control smart lights, locks, thermostats, and alarms. Both use weak encryption with hardcoded or easily derivable encryption keys. Researchers have demonstrated unlocking smart locks, disabling alarms, and controlling HVAC systems by replaying captured Zigbee frames. Physical proximity (10-100m) is all that's required — no internet access needed.

🛡

Defence: Network Segmentation

VLAN + Firewall Policy

The most effective IoT defence: put all IoT devices on a separate VLAN with no access to the corporate or personal network. A compromised smart thermostat cannot reach your NAS drive or your work laptop if they're on different VLANs with inter-VLAN routing blocked. Update firmware religiously, change all default credentials, and disable Bluetooth discovery when not actively pairing.

🔴 The Shodan Reality Check

Shodan is a search engine that indexes internet-connected devices. Right now, it shows over 1 million webcams, 500,000 routers, and 200,000 industrial control systems with default credentials or no authentication at all — fully accessible to anyone who searches. This is not theoretical. These are real devices, in real homes and businesses, waiting.
